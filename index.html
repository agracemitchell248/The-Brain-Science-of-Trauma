The Science

Your Brain on Job Loss

When you lost your job, your brain didn't just process it as disappointing news. It processed it as a threat to your survival.

🧠 The Survival Brain (Limbic System)

Deep in the brain. Its job: keep you alive, scan for threats, react instantly, in milliseconds, faster than conscious thought. It doesn't use words; it speaks through emotion, sensation, and urge.

When job loss hits, this system reads the loss of income, identity, and belonging as threats to survival, because for most of human history, they were.

💭 The Thinking Brain (Prefrontal Cortex)

Front of the brain. Its job: logic, planning, perspective, emotional regulation. It's slower, and it works in words and concepts, but it gets overwhelmed when the survival brain is too activated to let it do its job.

The Science

Why "Just Think Positive" Doesn't Work

When your survival brain detects a threat, it can override your thinking brain, in what's sometimes called an amygdala hijack. It's a feature, not a bug: facing a predator, you don't have time for careful analysis, so your brain gives the alarm system priority.

🔬 The Science

Stress hormones like cortisol reduce blood flow to your prefrontal cortex. That's why, under high stress, clear thinking, memory, focus, and seeing your options all get harder.

This is not weakness or failure. This is biology.

Recognize

The Rumination Trap

70 to 80% of people experience rumination after job loss. It's one of the most common, and most exhausting, responses.

Why your survival brain does this

It's trying to protect you by replaying the "threat," hoping to prevent it from happening again. It's stuck in a loop of "if I can just figure out what went wrong..."

But rumination doesn't solve anything. It keeps the stress response on, drains your energy, and keeps you stuck, even though your brain thinks it's helping.

The alternative: notice rumination as a signal from your survival brain, thank it for trying, then gently redirect your attention to something grounding in the present moment.

Your Nervous System

Threat Responses

⚡ Fight

Anger, irritability, the urge to push back. In job loss: defensiveness, picking fights, aggressive job searching.

🏃 Flight

Panic, anxiety, restlessness, the urge to avoid. In job loss: avoiding the search, networking anxiety, staying busy to outrun feelings.

🬦 Freeze

Numb, stuck, disconnected. In job loss: can't start applications, watching time pass, emotional numbness.

🤝 Fawn

The urge to appease or over-accommodate. In job loss: over-apologizing in interviews, accepting unfair offers, losing your own boundaries.

Key Concept

The Window of Tolerance

Developed by Dr. Dan Siegel, this describes the zone where your nervous system is regulated and you can function well: think clearly, manage emotion, respond flexibly. Outside it, everything gets harder.

Which zone do you find yourself in most often right now?

↑ Hyperarousal

Overwhelmed, anxious, racing thoughts, hypervigilant, reactive

→ Optimal Zone

Clear-headed, flexible, connected, can handle stress, grounded

↓ Hypoarousal

Numb, shut down, exhausted, disconnected, low energy

Job loss often pushes people outside their window, sometimes toward hyperarousal, sometimes hypoarousal, sometimes both. The goal isn't to never leave it (that's impossible). It's to notice when you have, understand what pushed you out, and have tools to guide yourself back.

Hope

Neuroplasticity & Hope

Your brain can change. Scientists once believed adult brains were fixed. We now know that's false.

Think of your brain like a forest: paths you walk often become well-worn trails; paths you avoid become overgrown. Every time you learn something new, practice a skill, or challenge an old pattern, you're building new neural trails and letting old ones fade.

You can create new paths.

Every time you notice dysregulation and use a tool to regulate, challenge a hard thought, or choose self-compassion over self-criticism, you're building new neural trails.

How UnBroken Works With Your Brain

Reset Exercises

Movement and sensation-based practices activate your vagus nerve, discharge stress energy, and help your prefrontal cortex come back online.

Name It Videos

Labeling emotions reduces amygdala activation. Dr. Dan Siegel calls it "name it to tame it."

Breathwork

Slow, deep breathing activates your parasympathetic ("rest and digest") system, one of the few ways you can directly influence it.

Emotion Check-ins

Build interoception (awareness of internal state) and space between stimulus and response.

You're not just managing symptoms. Every practice is training your brain to respond differently to stress: real, measurable, neurobiological change.
